Ionic bonding & electronegativity

Why ions stick together, how strongly, and how to tell an ionic bond from a covalent one.

Reading 8.1, 8.2, 8.4Wed, Nov 11Practice #1–18

Lewis symbols and the octet rule

Valence electrons are the outer-shell electrons used in bonding. A Lewis symbol is the element symbol with one dot per valence electron: one dot on each of the four sides first, then pair them up. Ca has 2 dots, B has 3, P has 5, Ne has 8.

Atoms gain, lose or share electrons until they have 8 valence electrons (a full s and p subshell). H, He, Li and Be follow the duet rule instead.

Ionic bonds

An ionic bond is the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, usually a metal and a nonmetal. Ionic solids are brittle and crystalline with high melting points.

  • Metals lose electrons to reach a noble-gas configuration; nonmetals gain them.
  • Predict formulas by balancing charge: Al³⁺ + F⁻ → AlF₃, Mg²⁺ + N³⁻ → Mg₃N₂, K⁺ + S²⁻ → K₂S.
  • Na loses one electron: Na [Ne]3s¹ → Na⁺ [Ne]. Na²⁺ never forms because the second electron would come from the inner core, which costs far too much energy.
  • Transition metals lose their 4s electrons first: Fe [Ar]3d⁶4s², Fe²⁺ [Ar]3d⁶, Fe³⁺ [Ar]3d⁵.

Lattice energy and Coulomb's law

Making NaCl from gaseous atoms looks unfavourable at first: removing Na's electron costs 496 kJ/mol (ionization energy) and adding one to Cl releases only 349 kJ/mol (electron affinity), a net cost of +147 kJ/mol. Ionic compounds are stable because of the huge attraction of the ions packed in the crystal, measured by the lattice energy.

NaCl(s) → Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g)   ΔHlattice = +788 kJ/mol
E ∝ Q₁Q₂ / d
  • Lattice energy rises as ion charges rise and as ions get smaller (shorter distance d).
  • Charge matters more than size. MgO (2+ × 2−, product 4) beats NaF (1+ × 1−, product 1).
  • Down a group the ions get bigger, so lattice energy falls: LiF > NaF > KF.
  • Larger lattice energy means a higher melting point.
  • Born–Haber cycles use lattice energy in Hess's-law calculations (goal 4).

Sign convention: ask your teacher

The Reading Notes define lattice energy as the energy needed to separate the solid into gaseous ions (positive, +788). The Day 1 slide defines it as the energy released when gaseous ions pack into the solid (negative). Same physics, opposite sign.

Electronegativity and bond polarity

Electronegativity is an atom's ability to pull shared electrons toward itself inside a molecule. It increases left to right across a period and decreases down a group. F is highest (4.0), Fr and Cs lowest (0.8).

ΔENBond typeExamples
less than 0.5Nonpolar covalent: equal sharingCl–Cl (0), C–H (0.4)
0.5 to 1.7Polar covalent: unequal sharingC–O (1.0), Mg–H (0.9), Al–Cl (1.5)
more than 1.7Ionic: electron transferNa–Cl (2.0), Cs–F (3.2)
  • The more electronegative atom gets δ⁻. In HF, F is δ⁻ and H is δ⁺. In Mg–H, H is δ⁻.
  • Ionic and covalent bonding are two ends of one continuum.
  • A dipole exists whenever equal and opposite charges are separated. Dipole moment = charge × distance. A larger ΔEN affects dipole moment more than bond length does, which is why HBr (ΔEN 0.7) has a larger dipole than HI (0.4).

Lewis structures & VSEPR

Drawing molecules, choosing the best structure, and predicting their 3D shape and polarity.

Reading 8.3, 8.5–8.8, 9.1–9.3Thu, Nov 12 & Fri, Nov 13Practice #19–27, 35–45

Covalent bonds

Covalent atoms share electron pairs. In a Lewis structure a shared pair is a line and a lone pair is two dots. Single, double and triple bonds share 1, 2 and 3 pairs; more shared pairs means a shorter, stronger bond.

Drawing Lewis structures

  1. Add up the valence electrons of every atom. Add one per negative charge, subtract one per positive charge.
  2. Put the least electronegative atom in the centre (never H) and connect everything with single bonds, 2 electrons each.
  3. Complete the octets of the outer atoms.
  4. Put leftover electrons on the central atom.
  5. If the central atom still lacks an octet, turn lone pairs into double or triple bonds.
FC = valence e⁻ − nonbonding e⁻ − ½(bonding e⁻)

The best structure has formal charges as close to zero as possible, with any negative formal charge on the more electronegative atom. Example: N in NH₄⁺ has 5 − 0 − ½(8) = +1.

Resonance

When two or more Lewis structures are equally good, the real molecule is their average, a resonance hybrid. Nitrate (NO₃⁻, 24 valence electrons) has three resonance structures; all three N–O bonds are really the same length, between a single and a double bond. The electrons are delocalized.

Exceptions to the octet rule

  • Odd number of electrons: NO, NO₂, ClO₂, O₂⁻.
  • Fewer than eight: BF₃ (B has 6), BeH₂ (Be has 4).
  • More than eight (period 3 and below only, where d orbitals exist): PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₂, SF₄.

Bond order and bond strength

Bond order is the number of shared pairs between two atoms: single 1, double 2, triple 3. Higher bond order means shorter and stronger. In a resonance hybrid the bond order is the average: carbonate's C–O bonds are 4/3 (four bonding pairs spread over three bonds).

VSEPR: electron domains repel

Each lone pair, single, double or triple bond counts as one electron domain. Domains spread as far apart as possible. The electron-domain geometry describes all domains; the molecular geometry describes only where the atoms are. Lone pairs take more room than bonding pairs and squeeze bond angles; in a trigonal bipyramid they sit in the equatorial positions.

Shape and polarity

A molecule's dipole is the vector sum of its bond dipoles. Polar bonds can cancel in a symmetric shape.

  • Nonpolar despite polar bonds: CO₂ (linear), BF₃ (trigonal planar), CCl₄ and CF₄ (tetrahedral), XeF₄ and KrF₄ (square planar), PCl₅, SF₆.
  • Polar: H₂O and H₂S (bent), NH₃ (pyramidal), SF₄ (seesaw), OCS (C=O and C=S dipoles are unequal, so they do not cancel).
  • Quick test: lone pairs on the central atom, or different outer atoms, usually make it polar. Exceptions are the symmetric lone-pair shapes: linear XeF₂ and square planar XeF₄.

Hybridization & bond order

Valence-bond theory: which orbitals overlap to make each bond, and how many σ and π bonds a molecule has.

Reading 9.4–9.8Mon, Nov 16Practice #23, 28–49

Orbital overlap

A bond forms when orbitals on two atoms overlap and two electrons of opposite spin share the space between the nuclei. More overlap means more electron density between the nuclei and a stronger bond. The bond length is the distance at the bottom of the potential-energy curve, where attraction and nucleus–nucleus repulsion balance; the depth of that minimum is the bond strength.

Hybrid orbitals

Atomic orbitals on one atom mix to make equivalent hybrid orbitals. The number of hybrids equals the number of orbitals mixed.

Steric number = σ bonds + lone pairs on the atom
Steric numberHybridizationOrbitals mixedGeometrys character
2sps + plinear, 180°50%
3sp²s + 2ptrigonal planar, 120°33%
4sp³s + 3ptetrahedral, 109.5°25%
5sp³ds + 3p + dtrigonal bipyramidal20%
6sp³d²s + 3p + 2doctahedral17%
  • Method: draw the Lewis structure, count domains with VSEPR, pick the matching hybrid set.
  • Shortcut: a carbon with only single bonds is sp³; one double bond, sp²; a triple bond or two doubles, sp.
  • sp³d and sp³d² need d orbitals, so only period 3 and below. Second-period atoms (C, N, O) never go beyond sp³.
  • More s character holds electrons closer to the nucleus, giving shorter bonds.

Sigma and pi bonds

  • σ (sigma): head-on overlap, electron density along the line between the nuclei. Every single bond is one σ bond.
  • π (pi): sideways overlap of unhybridized p orbitals, density above and below the axis. Weaker than σ.
  • Double bond = 1σ + 1π. Triple bond = 1σ + 2π. N≡N is 1σ + 2π.
  • Counting: ethene C₂H₄ has 5σ and 1π; HCN has 2σ and 2π; CO₂ has 2σ and 2π.

Molecular orbitals

Possibly not on the test

Goals 18–19 and Reading Notes 9.7–9.8 cover MO theory, but no class day taught it. Ask your teacher before spending time here.

  • Two atomic orbitals combine into two molecular orbitals: a lower-energy bonding MO and a higher-energy antibonding MO with little density between the nuclei.
  • Fill MOs like atomic orbitals: two electrons each with paired spins (Pauli), one per degenerate orbital before pairing (Hund).
Bond order = ½(bonding e⁻ − antibonding e⁻)
  • Paramagnetic: at least one unpaired electron; attracted to a magnet (O₂ is the classic example).
  • Diamagnetic: all electrons paired; weakly repelled.

Intermolecular forces

The attractions between molecules that set boiling points, vapor pressure, viscosity and solubility.

Reading 11.1–11.6Nov 16, 17, 18Practice #50–69

States of matter

A substance's state depends on the balance between the particles' kinetic energy (temperature) and the attractions between them. Gases have the weakest attractions, solids the strongest. Intermolecular forces act between molecules; intramolecular forces are the bonds inside a molecule. Boiling breaks intermolecular forces only.

The four forces

  • London dispersion (LDF): a momentary uneven electron cloud makes an instantaneous dipole that induces one in a neighbour. Present in every substance. Stronger with greater polarizability: bigger electron cloud, higher molar mass, more surface area (long chains beat branched ones).
  • Dipole–dipole: δ⁺ end of one polar molecule attracts the δ⁻ end of another. Stronger with more polarity.
  • Hydrogen bonding: H bonded directly to N, O or F, attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule. A very strong dipole–dipole force.
  • Ion–dipole: an ion and a polar molecule, such as Na⁺ surrounded by the δ⁻ oxygens of water.

Comparing two substances

Similar size and shape: polarity decides, and hydrogen bonding wins. Very different molar masses with no hydrogen bonding: the heavier one usually wins on LDF.

Properties of liquids

Stronger intermolecular forces give higher viscosity (resistance to flow), higher surface tension (energy to enlarge the surface), higher melting and boiling points, a higher critical temperature, and lower vapor pressure. Capillary action is the competition between adhesion to the tube and cohesion within the liquid. Like dissolves like: polar and hydrogen-bonding substances dissolve in water; nonpolar ones need nonpolar solvents.

Phase changes and vapor pressure

  • ΔHfus (melting) and ΔHvap (boiling) are endothermic; ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap.
  • On a heating curve the temperature stays flat during a phase change.
  • Vapor pressure is the pressure of vapor in dynamic equilibrium with its liquid. It depends only on temperature and intermolecular forces, not on how much liquid there is.
  • Volatile liquids (weak forces) have high vapor pressure and low boiling points.
  • A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the outside pressure. Normal boiling point = boiling point at 1 atm (760 torr).
  • Critical temperature: the highest temperature at which a liquid can exist. Critical pressure: the pressure needed to liquefy at that temperature. Above the critical point: supercritical fluid.

Phase diagrams

  • The vapor-pressure curve (liquid/gas) ends at the critical point; where it crosses 1 atm is the normal boiling point.
  • The sublimation curve separates solid and gas; the melting curve separates solid and liquid.
  • The melting curve leans slightly right for most substances (solid is denser). Water's leans left because ice is less dense than liquid water.
  • Triple point: all three phases in equilibrium.

Processing questions

1. n-pentane and 1-butanol have nearly the same molecular weight but very different Δt. Why?

1-butanol has an O–H group, so it hydrogen bonds in addition to LDF. n-pentane has only LDF. The stronger attractions in 1-butanol slow its evaporation, so its temperature drops only 1.7 °C against 16.1 °C for n-pentane.

2. Which alcohol has the strongest IMFs? The weakest?

Strongest: 1-butanol (smallest Δt, 1.7 °C). Weakest: methanol (largest Δt, 13.1 °C). All four hydrogen bond equally through one O–H, so the difference is LDF, which grows with the longer carbon chain and molecular weight.

3. Which alkane has the stronger IMFs?

n-hexane (Δt 12.0 °C) is stronger than n-pentane (16.1 °C). Both have only LDF; hexane is the larger molecule with more surface area and a bigger electron cloud.

Pre-lab: which liquids can hydrogen bond?

The four alcohols, because each has H bonded directly to O. The alkanes cannot: all their H atoms are bonded to C.

Procedure in brief

  • LabQuest set to collect for 240 s.
  • Wrap each probe in filter paper held by a rubber band.
  • Stand the probes in the liquids for at least 30 s.
  • Start collecting, then lift the probes out and tape them so they hang in the air.
  • Record the maximum (T₁) and minimum (T₂) temperatures and compute Δt.
  • Predict, then test, 1-butanol and n-pentane; then methanol and n-hexane.

Cheat sheet

The formulas, rules and rankings to have cold by Tuesday morning.

Bond type from ΔEN

  • Under 0.5: nonpolar covalent
  • 0.5 to 1.7: polar covalent
  • Over 1.7: ionic

EN rises across a period and up a group. F = 4.0 is the highest.

Lattice energy

E ∝ Q₁Q₂ / d
  • Bigger charges → larger (charge beats size)
  • Smaller ions → larger
  • Larger → higher melting point
  • NaCl: 788 kJ/mol

Formal charge

FC = V − N − ½B

V valence e⁻, N nonbonding e⁻, B bonding e⁻. Best structure: charges near zero, negative charge on the more electronegative atom.

Lewis steps

  • Count valence e⁻ (± charge)
  • Least EN atom in the centre, single bonds
  • Fill outer octets, leftovers on centre
  • Short of an octet? Make multiple bonds

Shapes to know

  • 2: linear 180°
  • 3: trigonal planar 120° · bent
  • 4: tetrahedral 109.5° · pyramidal 107° · bent 104.5°
  • 5: trig. bipyramidal · seesaw · T-shaped · linear
  • 6: octahedral · square pyramidal · square planar

Lone pairs: equatorial in 5, opposite each other in 6.

Hybridization

Steric no. = σ bonds + lone pairs
  • 2 sp · 3 sp² · 4 sp³ · 5 sp³d · 6 sp³d²
  • s character: sp 50%, sp² 33%, sp³ 25%
  • d hybrids: period 3 and below only

σ and π

  • Single = 1σ
  • Double = 1σ + 1π
  • Triple = 1σ + 2π
  • Higher bond order: shorter, stronger
  • Resonance: average the bond order (CO₃²⁻ = 4/3)
MO bond order = ½(b − a)

IMF ranking

LDF < dipole–dipole < H-bond < ion–dipole < ionic

  • LDF: in everything; grows with molar mass and surface area
  • H-bond: H on N, O or F

Stronger IMFs mean

  • Higher boiling and melting point
  • Higher viscosity and surface tension
  • Higher critical temperature, larger ΔHvap
  • Lower vapor pressure

Vapor pressure

  • Depends on temperature and IMFs only
  • Boils when VP = outside pressure
  • Normal bp: at 1 atm = 760 torr
  • ΔHsub = ΔHfus + ΔHvap
